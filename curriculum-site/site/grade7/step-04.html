Teach it — the concept

moves forward 100 cm until done does not guess. The motors are encoder motors: they count how far they have turned, and the block stops when the count says 100 cm. The robot has been measuring itself all along.

Contrast power with position. A command such as 50% power is like holding an accelerator halfway down: it says how strongly to drive a motor, not how fast it will actually go or where it should stop. Battery, friction and load all change the resulting speed. A plain DC motor has no sensor, so it cannot accept an angle — it simply keeps spinning until another block stops it. An encoder motor can accept a distance or angle because its sensor counts the rotation and gives the controller feedback about when the target has been reached.

The plain-motor block has an M1 / M2 / all menu. M1 controls the added DC motor plugged into extension port M1; M2 controls the one in port M2; all sends the same command to both ports at once. Do not confuse these with EM1 / EM2, which are the encoder-motor ports used by the two driving wheels: M means a plain extension motor, while EM means encoder motor.

So there are three numbers today, not one: what you asked for, what the ruler says, and what the robot thinks it did. When they disagree — and they will, because of wheel slip, battery level and floor surface — that gap is not a mistake. It is information, and every tuning decision for the rest of the year comes out of it.

Watch out
  • Tape the start line. "It worked last time" is nearly always a different starting spot.
  • Note the battery level beside each run. A robot that has driven all morning undershoots, and teams who do not record it will blame their code.
  • Do not let anyone average the three runs into one number yet. The spread is the finding.

Lesson flow — minute by minute

10′Add one drive block to yesterday's greeting. Park the robot on the start line and send it 100 cm.
newThe joystick counts as five separate events — up, down, left, right, and pressing it in. Each one can start a different script.
newDrives a measured distance and waits until it is done before the next block runs. What it actually travels is another matter — that is Step 2.
✓Push the joystick and the robot leaves the start line, stopping somewhere near 100 cm.
25′Three runs, same code. Measure each with a ruler and write all three down — not just the best one. They will differ, and that is the point.
✓Three different numbers written down — all three, not just the best one.
25′Change one thing: a different floor, or a lower speed. Three more runs. Which change mattered more?
✓Three more runs after changing one thing, and a stated answer to which change mattered more.
20′Put the robot's own count on the screen beside your ruler number. Now you can see what it believes versus what is true.
newSets the motor's counter back to zero, so the next reading measures from here rather than from power-on.
newWrites text at a fixed spot on the screen. The label number is a slot, so writing to label 1 again replaces that line instead of scrolling.
✓Two numbers you can see at once: what the robot believes it did, and what your ruler says it did.
15′Class data on the board. Same code, same floor, different robots — who drifted most? Argue about why.
✓Every team's numbers on the board, and an argument about why one robot drifts more than another.
5′Log the table. It is the reference every time the robot misbehaves this year.
✓The table copied into your log — the page you will turn back to every time the robot misbehaves.
