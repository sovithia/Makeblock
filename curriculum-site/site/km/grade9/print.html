Yesterday’s teaser, cashed in. A P-controller looks only at where the robot is: offset 40 gets the same correction whether the robot is racing back towards the line or drifting further off it. Those two situations need opposite treatment, and P cannot tell them apart — which is exactly why the trace still overshoots. The missing information is already in their data: the difference between this reading and the last one. Rising error means going the wrong way, push harder; falling error means the correction is already working, ease off. One subtraction, err − last_err, and the controller can finally see motion instead of position.

Then the discipline that makes it real. Freeze KP at Session 6’s champion value and change only KD — the one-change rule, now protecting a two-dimensional search that punishes sloppiness fast. Two things to predict aloud before anyone runs: too much D makes the robot twitchy rather than wobbly (it amplifies sensor noise, because noise is the fastest-changing thing in the signal), and the loop’s beat is part of the maths — err − last_err means nothing without a fixed interval between reads, so a stray print() inside the loop silently retunes the controller. That is the day’s real lesson and it applies to every control loop they will ever write.

បង្រៀន — គំនិតគោល

The Rover has no ultrasonic and no GPS. Everything it knows about where it is comes from counting how far its own motors have turned, and that is called dead reckoning — the same method that crossed oceans before satellites. The arithmetic is one line: a wheel of diameter D advances π·D for every full turn, so degrees convert to centimetres. But do not compute the constant from the sprocket — measure it. Command a long straight, lay a tape measure along what the robot actually did, and divide. Theory gives you a number; the floor gives you the right one, and on tracks those two are never the same because rubber slips.

Then the hard truth, and it is the one the capstone is built on: error accumulates and never cancels. Drive a 50 cm square four times round and measure the gap between where the robot started and where it finished — distance and heading, three runs, a spread rather than a number. Nothing is broken; skid steering scrubs the tracks sideways on every turn and a little of each turn is simply lost. That spread is the budget every later behaviour must live inside, and it is precisely why the mission ends by stopping on a colour marker rather than by counting centimetres. Dead reckoning gets you near. A sensor gets you there. Write that sentence on the board and leave it up until Session 20.

import mbot2, cyberpi, time DEG_PER_CM = 12.4 # MEASURED, not copied def turned(): return abs(mbot2.EM_get_angle('EM1')) def drive_cm(cm, speed=40): mbot2.EM_reset_angle('EM1') v = speed * (1 if cm > 0 else -1) while turned() < abs(cm) * DEG_PER_CM: mbot2.drive_speed(v, v) time.sleep(0.01) mbot2.EM_stop() for i in range(4): # the square again drive_cm(50) mbot2.turn(90)

បង្រៀន — គំនិតគោល

The Rover already has its arms — they went on in Grade 8 and they have not come off. What changes today is that there are two of them and the language is Python. Start ceremonial: open, hold, close, applause. Then meet the problem the second arm creates. The arms are mirror images, on S1 and S2, so "open" is a large angle on one and a small angle on the other — the same physical word, two opposite numbers. That asymmetry is a fact about the metal, and the whole job of today’s code is to make sure no other line in the program ever has to know it. grip_open() knows. Nothing else does.

Then the session’s bigger idea: defensive programming — code that assumes mistakes will happen and contains them. Grade 8 measured a safe range and respected it; this year the range is enforced, by a clamp() that every command passes through, so the robot is physically unable to hurt itself no matter what the rest of the file asks for. Test it adversarially and out loud: call arm_to(500, -500) on purpose and watch both arms stop quietly at their limits. Then hand the robot to another pair and invite them to break it, because a guard that has only been tested by the person who wrote it has not been tested.

import mbot2, time # measured by hand on THIS robot, power off # the arms mirror, so the numbers invert L_SHUT, L_OPEN = 20, 80 # left arm, S1 R_SHUT, R_OPEN = 80, 20 # right arm, S2 def clamp(v, a, b): lo, hi = min(a, b), max(a, b) return max(lo, min(hi, v)) # nothing reaches a servo except via here def arm_to(left, right): l = clamp(left, L_SHUT, L_OPEN) r = clamp(right, R_SHUT, R_OPEN) mbot2.servo_set(l, 'S1') mbot2.servo_set(r, 'S2') # the only place the mirror is spelled out def grip_open(): arm_to(L_OPEN, R_OPEN) def grip_close(): arm_to(L_SHUT, R_SHUT) grip_open() time.sleep(1) arm_to(500, -500) # clamped to the limits grip_close()

  • Servos are on S1 and S2 on this robot. S3 and S4 belong to a different build and a program that addresses them moves nothing at all: a silent failure, and worth naming before anyone loses ten minutes to it.
  • If an arm judders at the edges of its range, back both constants off 5°. Mechanical margin is wisdom.
  • Never force an arm past resistance. The gear teeth strip silently and there are no spares in the box.

បង្រៀន — គំនិតគោល

The challenge-ladder format: rungs of increasing difficulty, teams climb at their own pace, each rung demoed to the teacher before the next. Rung 1: close on a foam block and hold it while the robot is lifted and tilted by hand (grip angle tuning — too loose slips, too tight stalls the servo and heats it). Rung 2: grip, drive 30 cm with drive_cm(), release inside a taped zone — arm and motion coordinated, plus the discovery that driving shakes the payload and slower is surer. Rung 3: the same delivery with a 90° turn in the middle, released on a target within 3 cm — because a skid-steer turn scrubs sideways and the block rotates in the grip while it does.

One mechanical fact shapes the whole ladder and is worth stating before anyone climbs: each arm has exactly one servo, so grip and height are the same number. This robot cannot hold a block at one height and open at another — it releases where it stands. That rules out stacking entirely, and it is not a limitation to apologise for; it is the constraint every mission this year has to be designed around, which is what engineers do with constraints. Let the struggle at rung 3 happen — it is genuinely hard and that is its job. The debug discipline carries: one variable per attempt (grip angle OR approach speed OR turn speed), logged. Teams should end today knowing their grip’s personality: what it holds well, where it fumbles. That self-knowledge writes Session 12’s failure log.
ប្រយ័ត្ន

  • R2 zone generous (A5-size) today; R3 tightens it to a 3 cm target and the capstone tightens it again.
  • A servo held against a block it cannot close on gets hot: if an arm is warm to the touch, the grip angle is too far past contact. Back it off 5°.
  • Turn slowly while carrying. The block rotates in the grip during the scrub, and that is Session 9’s drift showing up in a new costume.

បង្រៀន — គំនិតគោល

Yesterday’s working sequences become a library: pick() (open, close the last centimetres, grip, settle), place() (release, retreat before turning), carry_to(cm) (a slower drive_cm, because a carried block slips). The capstone mission will then read like English: follow_line() … pick() … carry_to(30) … place(). When top-level code reads like the mission description, the decomposition is right — that’s the design smell to teach. Note what each verb is really hiding: pick() hides the mirrored servo angles from Session 10, carry_to() hides Session 9’s calibration constant. Three sessions of work, four words of code.

Second deliverable: the failure log. Run pick() twenty times; count successes; describe every failure precisely (‘block rotated in the grip during the turn’, not ‘it dropped it’). An 85% grip is fine — an unknown-% grip is a time bomb in a scored mission. Reliability engineering in one lunch-sized lesson: measure it, then decide whether to fix it or design around it (e.g. re-grip attempt on failure — which is Session 19’s recovery topic, seeded today).
Python (mBlock → របៀប Python)
def pick(): grip_open() # creep the last few centimetres drive_cm(6, speed=20) grip_close() time.sleep(0.3) # servos need a beat def place(): grip_open() time.sleep(0.2) # retreat BEFORE turning, or the arms # sweep the block out of the zone again drive_cm(-8, speed=20) def carry_to(cm): # slower: a carried block slips drive_cm(cm, speed=25)
ប្រយ័ត្ន
  • The sleep after grip_close matters — servos need a beat; its absence is the ghost bug of the week.
  • place() retreats before it turns. Skip that and the arms sweep the block out of the zone they just put it in: a bug that looks like a grip failure and is not.
  • Failure descriptions must name conditions; ‘sometimes’ is banned vocabulary.

បង្រៀន — គំនិតគោល

Integration day — the capstone in miniature, and deliberately so: line following (Session 6) + color-marker detection (Grade 7, Session 16’s idea, now in Python) + arm library (Session 12) chained into one mission. The new engineering content is the seams: stopping accurately AT the marker (momentum overshoots — the two-speed approach returns yet again), and positioning the arms relative to a block whose location is only as precise as the stop was. Errors compound down a pipeline; students meet that truth physically today — and Session 9’s measured error budget is the number that predicts how badly.

Run it as a milestone session: the pipeline working once, even slowly and inelegantly, is the win. Teams that get a clean run should immediately answer the harder question: how repeatable? Three runs in a row is the real bar — and the gap between ‘worked once’ and ‘works reliably’ is the theme of the entire build-sprint unit ahead.

ប្រយ័ត្ន
  • at_marker() and follow_step() are the class’s own helpers, not library calls — the top-level mission is nine lines because three sessions of work are hiding behind those two names. Point at that.
  • Buffer session — a struggling class can pre-place the block at a fixed, known stop point.
  • Slow the BASE speed near expected markers; detection at speed is flaky and that’s a real sensor lesson.

Session 13’s pipeline was a straight script — fine until something fails mid-mission and the program can’t express ‘go back and retry’. Introduce the state machine pattern: a state variable (SEARCHING / APPROACHING / PICKING / DELIVERING / DONE), a main loop that reads sensors and, depending on the current state, acts and decides transitions. It’s Grade 8’s mood variable wearing a hard hat — say exactly that; the recognition lands.

Before running: paper trace. One teammate is the CPU: finger on the current state, walk through a scenario (‘marker seen → state becomes PICKING’), including the nasty ones (‘what if the grip fails in PICKING?’ → they’ll want a retry transition — let them invent it). Tracing catches design holes in minutes that debugging would find in hours; it is also precisely how they’ll explain their code in the capstone review.

បង្រៀន — គំនិតគោល

Two workstreams. Robot: rehearse under Demo-Day conditions (official start, audience noise simulated, two attempts) and add cheap recovery where the run log points — the classics: re-grip once if pick fails (Session 12’s seed sprouts), re-find line if lost (Grade 7’s search behavior returns), timeout-and-skip rather than freeze forever. Then freeze the configuration — the Grade 7 race-config ritual, now second nature.

Pitch (5 minutes, strict): the problem (30 s) → the solution & how it works (2′) → live demo (90 s) → one honest failure story and what it taught (1′). The failure story is mandatory and rubric-weighted — it’s where engineering maturity shows, and after three years of debug discipline every team has a good one. Rehearse with a timer; cutting to fit five minutes is itself the exercise. Assign demo roles: driver (starts the run), narrator (talks over it), catcher (handles the robot if chaos wins).
ប្រយ័ត្ន
  • Buffer session: recovery can shrink to timeout-and-skip only — but the freeze and the timed rehearsal are untouchable.
  • The failure story must end on the lesson, not the excuse — coach the last sentence.

① ពិនិត្យឡើងវិញ និងកែកំហុស 10′

Let the run log choose the recovery. Do not build the one that sounds most impressive — build the one the failure data justifies: re-grip once, re-find the line, or time out and skip. The log names which, and building the wrong one spends a whole session solving a problem this robot does not have.

④ ពង្រីក — តាមស្តង់ដារ 25′
  • recovery triggers on demand Cause the failure deliberately and watch the recovery fire. A recovery never seen to trigger is a recovery nobody should count on.
  • frozen, in writing The race configuration written out in full, with no edits after that line.
  • 5:00 twice, all four parts Problem, solution, live demo, and the honest failure story — timed, twice.
⑤ កំណត់ហេតុវិស្វកម្ម 10′

The frozen configuration and the pitch script with timings per section. The failure story is rubric-weighted, so its last sentence is worth drafting rather than improvising — it has to end on the lesson, never on the excuse.

⑥ ការត្រួតពិនិត្យការរចនា 15′

Peers give one keep note and one cut note per pitch. Criteria — does the failure story end on what was learned, and did the live demo fit inside its ninety seconds without anyone having to rush?

Run it as the event three years earned: audience seated (invite classes, teachers, parents if possible), visible scoreboard (phase points per team’s own success criteria + small style bonus), two attempts each with pit time between, pitches delivered from the front. The teacher’s job today is master of ceremonies — the students carry the content, and they can.

Close the arc deliberately. The retrospective ritual: the Session 1/Grade 7 question projected (‘what is a robot?’) — let them answer it again, now with three years of hands. Then the pathways talk, concrete and short: what they built maps to real fields — control engineering (their P-controller), ML engineering (their mirror), embedded software (their guard functions), robotics competitions, and the kit’s 30+ module ecosystem for a robotics club afterlife. End on the honest sentence: they didn’t learn about robotics — they did robotics, sixty hours of it.
